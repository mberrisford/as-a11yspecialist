PDFs are everywhere. We use them for reports, forms, and manuals. But are the PDFs you share usable by everyone? PDF accessibility ensures that people with disabilities can read and interact with your documents. Let's look at what that means.

## What Is PDF Accessibility?

PDF accessibility means making sure your PDF documents can be used by people with disabilities. This includes people who are blind and use screen readers, have low vision, or have mobility or cognitive impairments.

An accessible PDF is structured. It works with assistive technologies like screen readers and text-to-speech software. Without a proper structure, a PDF might just be a flat image of text, making it unreadable to these tools.

## Why It Matters

Creating accessible documents is not just a technical task. It is about inclusion. When PDFs are not accessible, you exclude people from accessing information, completing forms, or engaging with your content. It creates a barrier that prevents equal participation.

In many places, providing accessible documents is also a legal requirement. But beyond compliance, it is the right thing to do.

## Key Features of an Accessible PDF

So what makes a PDF accessible? It comes down to a few key things that assistive technologies need to interpret the content.

### Tagged Content

Tags provide a hidden layer of code that gives structure to the document. They tell a screen reader what is a heading, a paragraph, a list, or an image. Without tags, a screen reader sees only a wall of unorganized text. It's similar to semantic HTML in that regard.

### Logical Reading Order

The reading order defines the sequence in which a screen reader reads the content. For complex layouts with columns or sidebars, setting the correct reading order is essential for the document to make sense.

### Alternative Text for Images

Images, charts, and graphs need alternative text, or alt text. This text describes the visual information for someone who cannot see it. The description should be concise and communicate the purpose of the image. Decorative images should be marked as "artifacs" so they are completely ignored by assistive technologies.

### Navigational Aids

Features like a table of contents, bookmarks, and descriptive links help all users navigate the document. For screen reader users, these aids are vital for finding information without having to read the entire document from the beginning.

## A Simple Checklist

You do not need to be a technical expert to check for basic accessibility. Before you share a PDF, ask yourself these questions:

- Can I select text with my cursor? If not, it is likely an image and is not accessible.
- Does the document have clear headings and a logical structure?
- Do images and charts have text descriptions?
- Are links descriptive? "Read our report on accessibility" is better than a generic "click here."
- Does the file have a title in the document properties?

## Conclusion

PDF accessibility is a core part of digital inclusion. It ensures that everyone has equal access to information. By focusing on structure, text alternatives, and navigation, you can create documents that are usable for a wider audience. It is a small effort that makes a big difference.
